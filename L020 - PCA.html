20.1 Unsupervised Learning and Dimensionality Reduction

Everything we have studied so far in this course falls under the heading of supervised learning: we are given sample points $(\boldsymbol{x}^{(i)}, y^{(i)})$ paired with labels, and our goal is to learn a mapping from inputs to outputs. In this chapter we step outside that paradigm entirely. In unsupervised learning, we have sample points $\boldsymbol{x}^{(1)}, \ldots, \boldsymbol{x}^{(n)} \in \mathbb{R}^d$ with no labels whatsoever — no classes, no regression targets, no $y$ values. Without a target to predict, the goal shifts: we want to discover latent structure in the data itself.

This may seem unmotivated at first — what is the point of studying data if there is nothing to predict? But many of the richest datasets in science, medicine, and industry arrive without labels. A genomics lab measures 300,000 genetic variants per person but has no labeled outcomes. A photographer has a million face images but no annotations. A company has years of sensor readings from its machinery but has never systematically labeled which readings preceded failure. In all these cases, unsupervised learning provides tools for organizing, compressing, and understanding the data.

20.1.1 Three Forms of Unsupervised Learning

Definition · Unsupervised Learning

Given $n$ sample points $\boldsymbol{x}^{(1)}, \ldots, \boldsymbol{x}^{(n)} \in \mathbb{R}^d$ with no associated labels, the three principal forms of unsupervised learning are:

  • Clustering: partition the $n$ points into $k$ groups such that points within a group are more similar to each other than to points in other groups. The number $k$ may be specified or inferred.
  • Dimensionality reduction: find a mapping from $\mathbb{R}^d$ to $\mathbb{R}^k$ with $k \ll d$ that preserves as much structure (typically variance or pairwise distances) as possible.
  • Density estimation: fit a probability density $p(\boldsymbol{x})$ to the sample points, enabling probability queries, sampling, and anomaly detection.

Clustering and dimensionality reduction are, in a sense, opposites. Clustering is discrete: it assigns each point to one of a finite number of categories, collapsing continuous variation into group membership. Dimensionality reduction is continuous: it identifies a low-dimensional manifold or subspace and maps each point to a coordinate on that manifold, preserving gradual variation from point to point. We focus on dimensionality reduction in this chapter and specifically on its most famous instance: Principal Components Analysis (PCA), invented by Karl Pearson in 1901.

20.1.2 Why Reduce Dimensionality?

High-dimensional data is common in modern machine learning. MNIST handwritten digits are $28 \times 28 = 784$-dimensional vectors; natural images are millions of pixels; gene expression datasets have tens of thousands of features per sample. Dimensionality reduction is valuable for several reasons.

MotivationDescriptionExample
Computational speedAlgorithms run faster on lower-dimensional data; regression is $O(d^3)$ in $d$Reducing 784-D MNIST to 50-D speeds up SVM by a factor of $\sim 30$
Overfitting reductionRemoving irrelevant dimensions reduces the effective model complexity, analogous to feature selectionPCA preprocessing before regression on high-$d$, low-$n$ datasets
Visualization2-D or 3-D projections enable visual inspection of structureMNIST digit clusters visible in 2-D PCA plot
Basis discoveryThe PCA basis reveals the principal modes of variation in the dataEigenfaces, gene expression modes, climate indices
Data Science Application · PCA as a Preprocessor

A common workflow is: run PCA on the design matrix $X$, project all examples (training and test) into principal component space retaining $k$ components, then run a downstream supervised algorithm (regression, SVM, neural network) in that $k$-dimensional space. The key property PCA exploits is that the directions of lowest eigenvalue tend to capture noise rather than signal, so removing them simultaneously reduces dimension and denoises. However, there is a caveat discussed in Section 20.6: the directions of lowest variance are not always the directions of lowest predictive relevance. When the labels are available, supervised dimensionality reduction methods (e.g., Linear Discriminant Analysis) may be preferable.

20.2 Orthogonal Projection

The mathematical machinery of PCA rests on a single geometric operation: orthogonal projection. Before we can understand why eigenvectors are the right choice of principal directions, we need to establish clearly what it means to project a data point onto a vector or a subspace, and how to compute it efficiently.

Throughout this chapter, $X \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d}$ denotes the centered design matrix — that is, we have already subtracted the sample mean $\bar{\boldsymbol{x}} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \boldsymbol{x}^{(i)}$ from every row. Centering ensures the data has zero mean, which simplifies the variance calculations to follow. We will continue to call this matrix $X$ (rather than introducing new notation) with the understanding that centering has already been applied.

20.2.1 Projection onto a Unit Vector

Definition · Orthogonal Projection onto a Vector

Let $\boldsymbol{w} \in \mathbb{R}^d$ and $\boldsymbol{x} \in \mathbb{R}^d$. The orthogonal projection of $\boldsymbol{x}$ onto the line spanned by $\boldsymbol{w}$ is: $$\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}} = \frac{\boldsymbol{x} \cdot \boldsymbol{w}}{\|\boldsymbol{w}\|^2}\,\boldsymbol{w} = \frac{\boldsymbol{w}\boldsymbol{w}^\top}{\|\boldsymbol{w}\|^2}\,\boldsymbol{x}.$$ When $\boldsymbol{w}$ is a unit vector ($\|\boldsymbol{w}\| = 1$), this simplifies to $\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}} = (\boldsymbol{x} \cdot \boldsymbol{w})\,\boldsymbol{w}$. The scalar $\boldsymbol{x} \cdot \boldsymbol{w}$ is the signed projection length (positive if $\boldsymbol{x}$ and $\boldsymbol{w}$ point in the same half-space, negative otherwise).

Geometrically, the projection $\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}$ is the unique point on the line through $\boldsymbol{w}$ (and the origin) that minimizes the distance $\|\boldsymbol{x} - \tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}\|$. The vector $\boldsymbol{x} - \tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}$ is perpendicular to $\boldsymbol{w}$, which is why it is called an orthogonal projection. The unit-vector formula $\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}} = (\boldsymbol{x} \cdot \boldsymbol{w})\boldsymbol{w}$ is the one to remember; when $\boldsymbol{w}$ is not a unit vector, normalize first: replace $\boldsymbol{w}$ by $\boldsymbol{w}/\|\boldsymbol{w}\|$.

Figure 20.1. Orthogonal projection of a point $\mathbf{x}$ (green) onto the unit vector $\mathbf{w}$ (navy, direction shown by blue line). The projected point $\tilde{\mathbf{x}} = (\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{w})\mathbf{w}$ is shown in red. The gold dashed line is the perpendicular from $\mathbf{x}$ to $\tilde{\mathbf{x}}$, with the right-angle marker confirming orthogonality. The key takeaway: the projection $\tilde{\mathbf{x}}$ is the closest point on the line to $\mathbf{x}$.

20.2.2 Projection onto a $k$-Dimensional Subspace

PCA typically projects onto a $k$-dimensional subspace for $k > 1$. This is straightforward when the basis of the subspace is an orthonormal set — that is, a set of $k$ vectors $\boldsymbol{v}_1, \ldots, \boldsymbol{v}_k$ satisfying $\boldsymbol{v}_i \cdot \boldsymbol{v}_j = \delta_{ij}$ (Kronecker delta: 1 if $i=j$, 0 otherwise).

Definition · Orthogonal Projection onto a $k$-Dimensional Subspace

Let $\boldsymbol{v}_1, \ldots, \boldsymbol{v}_k \in \mathbb{R}^d$ be orthonormal ($\boldsymbol{v}_i^\top\boldsymbol{v}_j = \delta_{ij}$) and let $V = [\boldsymbol{v}_1 \mid \cdots \mid \boldsymbol{v}_k] \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times k}$. The orthogonal projection of $\boldsymbol{x}$ onto the subspace $\text{span}(\boldsymbol{v}_1,\ldots,\boldsymbol{v}_k)$ is: $$\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}} = \sum_{i=1}^{k}(\boldsymbol{x} \cdot \boldsymbol{v}_i)\,\boldsymbol{v}_i = VV^\top\boldsymbol{x}.$$ The matrix $P = VV^\top \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ is the orthogonal projection matrix onto this subspace; it satisfies $P^2 = P$ (idempotent) and $P^\top = P$ (symmetric).

The formula is simply a sum of one-dimensional projections, one per basis vector. This works without cross-terms precisely because the $\boldsymbol{v}_i$'s are mutually orthogonal. If the basis were not orthogonal, a more complex formula involving a Gram matrix would be required. This is why PCA always works with an orthonormal basis — it makes the projection formula as simple as possible.

20.2.3 Principal Coordinates

Definition · Principal Coordinates

Given orthonormal principal directions $\boldsymbol{v}_1, \ldots, \boldsymbol{v}_k$ and a sample point $\boldsymbol{x}$, the principal coordinates of $\boldsymbol{x}$ are the $k$ dot products: $$z_i = \boldsymbol{x} \cdot \boldsymbol{v}_i, \quad i = 1, \ldots, k.$$ The vector $\boldsymbol{z} = (z_1, \ldots, z_k) \in \mathbb{R}^k$ is the representation of $\boldsymbol{x}$ in principal component space. In matrix notation, for the full design matrix: $Z = XV \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times k}$.

There is a subtle but important distinction between two outputs of PCA: the projected point $\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}} = VV^\top\boldsymbol{x} \in \mathbb{R}^d$ (still a $d$-dimensional vector, living in the original feature space on the subspace) and the principal coordinates $\boldsymbol{z} = V^\top\boldsymbol{x} \in \mathbb{R}^k$ (a $k$-dimensional vector in the new principal component space). For downstream machine learning tasks, it is the principal coordinates $\boldsymbol{z}$ that we pass as features; for visualization and reconstruction tasks, we may want $\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}$.

Intuition · Two Representations of the Same Projection

Think of a shadow cast by a lamp. The principal coordinates $\boldsymbol{z} = V^\top\boldsymbol{x}$ tell you where on the wall the shadow lands — they live in the 2D coordinate system of the wall. The projected point $\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}} = V\boldsymbol{z}$ is the same shadow, but described using the original 3D room coordinates. Both describe the same geometric object; the choice between them depends on whether you need to stay in the original feature space (e.g., to visualize alongside other data) or in the compressed space (e.g., to run a faster algorithm).

20.3 The PCA Algorithm

With the projection machinery established, we can write down the complete PCA algorithm. Its inputs are a design matrix and a choice of target dimension $k$; its outputs are the principal directions and either the principal coordinates or the projected design matrix.

20.3.1 Centering and Normalization

Definition · The PCA Algorithm

Given a design matrix $X \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d}$ (rows = samples, columns = features) and target dimension $k \leq d$:

  1. Center: compute $\bar{\boldsymbol{x}} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \boldsymbol{x}^{(i)}$ and replace each row by $\boldsymbol{x}^{(i)} - \bar{\boldsymbol{x}}$. Call the result $X$ (overwriting notation). Now $\sum_i \boldsymbol{x}^{(i)} = \boldsymbol{0}$.
  2. Optionally normalize: divide each column $j$ by its sample standard deviation $s_j = \sqrt{\frac{1}{n}\sum_i (x_{ij})^2}$. This should be done when features have different units of measurement; omit it when all features share the same units.
  3. Form $X^\top X$: compute the $d \times d$ matrix $X^\top X$ (proportional to the sample covariance $\hat{\Sigma} = \frac{1}{n}X^\top X$).
  4. Eigendecompose: compute eigenvalues $\lambda_1 \leq \lambda_2 \leq \cdots \leq \lambda_d$ (sorted ascending) and corresponding orthonormal eigenvectors $\boldsymbol{v}_1, \ldots, \boldsymbol{v}_d$ of $X^\top X$.
  5. Select $k$: choose the $k$ eigenvectors with the largest eigenvalues: $\boldsymbol{v}_{d}, \boldsymbol{v}_{d-1}, \ldots, \boldsymbol{v}_{d-k+1}$. Form $V_k = [\boldsymbol{v}_{d-k+1} \mid \cdots \mid \boldsymbol{v}_d] \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times k}$.
  6. Project: compute principal coordinates $Z = XV_k \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times k}$ and/or projected points $\tilde{X} = XV_kV_k^\top \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d}$.

Warning · Centering is Mandatory; Normalization Requires Judgment

Centering is always required. Without it, the first principal component would be dominated by the direction of the sample mean rather than the direction of maximum spread. Normalization, however, requires domain knowledge. If features represent image pixel intensities (same unit, same scale) do not normalize individually — this would amplify noise in low-intensity regions relative to high-intensity ones. If features represent a city's population (millions) and its crime rate (per 100,000) in the same model, normalization is essential; otherwise, PCA will align almost entirely with the population axis due to its vastly larger magnitude.

20.3.2 Eigendecomposition and Component Selection

The matrix $X^\top X$ is symmetric and positive semidefinite, so by the spectral theorem all eigenvalues are real and non-negative, and the eigenvectors can be chosen to be orthonormal. This is not a coincidence — it is what makes PCA tractable. For any other matrix, eigenvalues could be complex and the eigenvectors non-orthogonal.

Theorem · Spectral Theorem for $X^\top X$

For any centered design matrix $X \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d}$, the matrix $M = X^\top X$ is symmetric positive semidefinite: $M = M^\top$ and $\boldsymbol{u}^\top M \boldsymbol{u} \geq 0$ for all $\boldsymbol{u} \in \mathbb{R}^d$. Therefore:

  • All eigenvalues $\lambda_j$ of $M$ are real and $\lambda_j \geq 0$.
  • Eigenvectors corresponding to distinct eigenvalues are orthogonal.
  • There exists an orthonormal basis of $\mathbb{R}^d$ consisting of eigenvectors of $M$: $M = V \Lambda V^\top$ where $V = [\boldsymbol{v}_1 \cdots \boldsymbol{v}_d]$ is orthogonal and $\Lambda = \text{diag}(\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_d)$.

Proof Sketch

Symmetry: $(X^\top X)^\top = X^\top(X^\top)^\top = X^\top X$. Positive semidefiniteness: $\boldsymbol{u}^\top(X^\top X)\boldsymbol{u} = \|X\boldsymbol{u}\|^2 \geq 0$ for all $\boldsymbol{u}$. Real eigenvalues and orthogonal eigenvectors follow from the real symmetric spectral theorem (a standard result in linear algebra). The decomposition $M = V\Lambda V^\top$ is the eigendecomposition, which exists for any real symmetric matrix. □

Example · PCA on a 3D Anisotropic Dataset

Setup. Suppose $n=200$ points in $\mathbb{R}^3$ (already centered) have sample covariance $\hat\Sigma = \frac{1}{n}X^\top X = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 2 & 0 \\ 2 & 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0.1\end{pmatrix}$.

Eigendecomposition. The $x_3$ dimension is nearly independent (covariance 0 with $x_1, x_2$) and has tiny variance (0.1). The block $\begin{pmatrix}3&2\\2&2\end{pmatrix}$ has eigenvalues $\lambda = \frac{5 \pm \sqrt{9}}{2}$, giving $\lambda_1 = 1$ and $\lambda_2 = 4$. With the third eigenvalue $\lambda_3 = 0.1$, sorting descending: $\lambda_d = 4 \geq \lambda_{d-1} = 1 \geq \lambda_{d-2} = 0.1$.

Selection (k=2).} We keep the top 2 eigenvectors, capturing variance $(4+1)/(4+1+0.1) \approx 98\%$. The $x_3$ direction contributes only 2% of the variance and is discarded. The algorithm effectively identifies that the data lies nearly in a 2D plane.

Interpretation. The 200 examples are projected from $\mathbb{R}^3$ to $\mathbb{R}^2$ with less than 2% variance loss. This is a substantial dimensionality reduction with minimal information loss.

20.3.3 Choosing $k$: The Scree Plot

A key practical question is: how many principal components should we retain? One principled approach is to plot cumulative explained variance as a function of $k$.

Definition · Explained Variance Ratio

Let $\lambda_1 \leq \cdots \leq \lambda_d$ be the eigenvalues of $X^\top X$. The proportion of variance explained by the top $k$ principal components is: $$\text{EVR}(k) = \frac{\sum_{j=d-k+1}^{d} \lambda_j}{\sum_{j=1}^{d} \lambda_j} = \frac{\lambda_{d-k+1} + \cdots + \lambda_d}{\lambda_1 + \cdots + \lambda_d}.$$ A scree plot displays $\text{EVR}(k)$ (or sometimes each individual $\lambda_j$) as a function of $k$. The sum of all eigenvalues equals the total variance: $\sum_j \lambda_j = \text{tr}(X^\top X) = \sum_j \|\boldsymbol{x}^{(j)}\|^2$.

Figure 20.3. Scree plot for a simulated 17-dimensional dataset. Blue bars show the variance contributed by each additional principal component; the red curve shows cumulative explained variance. The gold dashed line marks the 90% threshold (requiring ~8 components). The green dotted line marks 73% at $k=3$, meaning just three components (out of 17) capture nearly three-quarters of the total variance — a hallmark of data with strong low-dimensional structure. In practice, practitioners often choose $k$ at the "elbow" of the curve or where EVR exceeds a threshold (e.g., 90%).

The scree plot provides a visual guide, but cross-validation (as discussed in Section 7.3) gives a more rigorous data-driven criterion: embed training data to $k$ dimensions, run a downstream task (regression, classification), and choose $k$ to minimize dev-set error. The scree-plot approach is model-free and applies even when there is no downstream task.

Figure 20.6. Simulated PCA projection of MNIST handwritten digits to 2D principal component space. Each color represents one digit class (0–7). Despite projecting from 784 dimensions to just 2, significant class separation is visible: the digit-0 cluster (red) and digit-1 cluster (orange) are broadly separated from each other. Classes that look visually similar (e.g., 3 and 5) overlap more in the projection. This illustrates both the power of PCA (global structure is preserved) and its limitation (fine discriminative structure may be lost).

20.4 PCA Derivation I: Maximum Projected Variance

So far we have stated that PCA chooses eigenvectors of $X^\top X$ as principal directions, and described the algorithm. But why eigenvectors? What optimization problem do they solve? In this section we give the first of two rigorous derivations that answer this question. The goal: find the unit vector $\boldsymbol{w} \in \mathbb{R}^d$ such that projecting the data onto $\boldsymbol{w}$ preserves as much variance as possible.

The intuition is that variance captures information: a direction with high variance is one where data points are spread out, meaning the projection distinguishes well between different points. A direction with zero variance is one where all projected points coincide — projecting onto it tells us nothing about the data. We therefore want to maximize variance. This derivation connects directly to Gaussian density estimation: fitting a Gaussian to the data by MLE gives $\hat\Sigma = \frac{1}{n}X^\top X$, and the eigenvectors of $\hat\Sigma$ are exactly the principal axes of the fitted Gaussian ellipse.

20.4.1 The Rayleigh Quotient

Definition · Rayleigh Quotient

For a symmetric matrix $M \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ and nonzero vector $\boldsymbol{w} \in \mathbb{R}^d$, the Rayleigh quotient is: $$R(M, \boldsymbol{w}) = \frac{\boldsymbol{w}^\top M \boldsymbol{w}}{\boldsymbol{w}^\top \boldsymbol{w}} = \frac{\boldsymbol{w}^\top M \boldsymbol{w}}{\|\boldsymbol{w}\|^2}.$$ It satisfies $\lambda_{\min}(M) \leq R(M,\boldsymbol{w}) \leq \lambda_{\max}(M)$ for all $\boldsymbol{w} \neq \boldsymbol{0}$, with equality when $\boldsymbol{w}$ is the corresponding eigenvector.

The connection to PCA: the sample variance of the projected data $\{(\boldsymbol{x}^{(i)} \cdot \boldsymbol{w})\}_{i=1}^n$ (using centered $X$ so the mean of projections is zero) is: $$\hat\sigma^2_{\boldsymbol{w}} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n (\boldsymbol{x}^{(i)} \cdot \boldsymbol{w})^2 = \frac{1}{n}\|X\boldsymbol{w}\|^2 = \frac{\boldsymbol{w}^\top X^\top X\, \boldsymbol{w}}{n\,\|\boldsymbol{w}\|^2} \cdot \|\boldsymbol{w}\|^2.$$ For a unit vector $\|\boldsymbol{w}\|=1$ this is simply $\boldsymbol{w}^\top X^\top X\,\boldsymbol{w}/n$, and maximizing it over all unit $\boldsymbol{w}$ is equivalent to maximizing the Rayleigh quotient $R(X^\top X, \boldsymbol{w})$.

PCA Variance Criterion
$$\boldsymbol{w}^* = \operatorname*{arg\,max}_{\boldsymbol{w} \neq \boldsymbol{0}} R(X^\top X,\, \boldsymbol{w}) = \operatorname*{arg\,max}_{\|\boldsymbol{w}\|=1} \boldsymbol{w}^\top X^\top X\, \boldsymbol{w}$$

20.4.2 Maximization via Eigenvectors

Theorem · Rayleigh Quotient Maximization

Let $M \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ be real symmetric with eigenvalues $\lambda_1 \leq \cdots \leq \lambda_d$ and corresponding orthonormal eigenvectors $\boldsymbol{v}_1, \ldots, \boldsymbol{v}_d$. Then: $$\max_{\boldsymbol{w} \neq \boldsymbol{0}} R(M, \boldsymbol{w}) = \lambda_d, \quad \text{achieved uniquely (up to sign) at } \boldsymbol{w} = \boldsymbol{v}_d.$$ More generally, subject to $\boldsymbol{w} \perp \boldsymbol{v}_d, \ldots, \boldsymbol{v}_{d-j+2}$: $$\max_{\boldsymbol{w} \perp \text{span}(\boldsymbol{v}_d,\ldots,\boldsymbol{v}_{d-j+2})} R(M, \boldsymbol{w}) = \lambda_{d-j+1}, \quad \text{achieved at } \boldsymbol{w} = \boldsymbol{v}_{d-j+1}.$$

Proof

Since $\{\boldsymbol{v}_1, \ldots, \boldsymbol{v}_d\}$ is an orthonormal basis of $\mathbb{R}^d$, write $\boldsymbol{w} = \sum_{j=1}^d c_j \boldsymbol{v}_j$ with $\sum_j c_j^2 = \|\boldsymbol{w}\|^2$. Then $M\boldsymbol{w} = \sum_j c_j \lambda_j \boldsymbol{v}_j$ and $\boldsymbol{w}^\top M \boldsymbol{w} = \sum_j c_j^2 \lambda_j$. Hence: $$R(M,\boldsymbol{w}) = \frac{\sum_j c_j^2 \lambda_j}{\sum_j c_j^2}.$$ This is a weighted average of the eigenvalues $\lambda_j$, with non-negative weights $c_j^2/\|\boldsymbol{w}\|^2$ summing to 1. A weighted average is always at most the largest term: $R(M,\boldsymbol{w}) \leq \lambda_d$. Equality holds iff all weight is on $j=d$, i.e., $c_j = 0$ for $j \neq d$, i.e., $\boldsymbol{w} \propto \boldsymbol{v}_d$. The constrained case follows by the same argument restricted to the orthogonal complement. □

The proof is elegant: PCA finds the direction that maximizes projected variance because the eigenvector with maximum eigenvalue places all the "weight" on the largest eigenvalue in the Rayleigh quotient expansion. Any other direction mixes in smaller eigenvalues and hence achieves smaller variance.

20.4.3 Extension to $k$ Components

The full PCA result follows immediately from the constrained maximization: to find the best $k$-dimensional subspace for projecting, we greedily select the top $k$ eigenvectors. The $j$-th principal direction $\boldsymbol{v}_{d-j+1}$ maximizes projected variance among all directions orthogonal to the first $j-1$ principal directions. Together, these $k$ directions span the $k$-dimensional subspace of maximum projected variance.

Data Science Application · PCA and the Sample Covariance Matrix

The MLE estimate of the covariance of a Gaussian fit to centered data is $\hat\Sigma = \frac{1}{n}X^\top X$. Since $X^\top X = n\hat\Sigma$, the eigenvectors of $X^\top X$ are identical to those of $\hat\Sigma$, and the eigenvalues scale by $n$. Thus PCA can equivalently be described as finding the principal axes of the fitted Gaussian density — the directions along which the Gaussian has greatest spread. This is Derivation I from the lecture: a heuristic motivation based on maximum likelihood Gaussian fitting, which the Rayleigh quotient argument here makes rigorous.

20.5 PCA Derivation II: Minimum Reconstruction Error

The maximum-variance criterion provides one justification for PCA. A second, complementary justification comes from asking a different question: which $k$-dimensional subspace minimizes the average distance from each data point to its projection? This is a reconstruction error perspective — we are looking for the subspace that best approximates all the data points simultaneously, in the sense of minimizing total squared distance from each point to the subspace.

This perspective is analogous to least-squares linear regression, but with an important twist: in regression there is a privileged response direction $y$ and we measure error only in that direction. In PCA there is no privileged direction; all feature directions are treated equally, and we measure error as the perpendicular distance from each point to the subspace. This difference, though seemingly minor, completely changes the mathematical structure of the problem.

20.5.1 The Projection Distance Objective

Definition · Mean Squared Projection Distance

Given centered data $\boldsymbol{x}^{(1)}, \ldots, \boldsymbol{x}^{(n)}$ and a unit vector $\boldsymbol{w} \in \mathbb{R}^d$, the mean squared projection distance to the line spanned by $\boldsymbol{w}$ is: $$J(\boldsymbol{w}) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|\boldsymbol{x}^{(i)} - \tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}^{(i)}\|^2 = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\|\boldsymbol{x}^{(i)} - (\boldsymbol{x}^{(i)}\cdot\boldsymbol{w})\boldsymbol{w}\right\|^2.$$ PCA (for $k=1$) selects $\boldsymbol{w}^* = \operatorname*{arg\,min}_{\|\boldsymbol{w}\|=1} J(\boldsymbol{w})$.

20.5.2 Equivalence to Maximum Variance

Theorem · Equivalence of the Two PCA Criteria

For centered data with $\frac{1}{n}\sum_i \boldsymbol{x}^{(i)} = \boldsymbol{0}$ and unit vector $\boldsymbol{w}$: $$J(\boldsymbol{w}) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n\|\boldsymbol{x}^{(i)}\|^2 - \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n(\boldsymbol{x}^{(i)}\cdot\boldsymbol{w})^2 = C - \hat\sigma^2_{\boldsymbol{w}}$$ where $C = \frac{1}{n}\sum_i \|\boldsymbol{x}^{(i)}\|^2$ is a constant (the total variance) independent of $\boldsymbol{w}$. Therefore: $$\operatorname*{arg\,min}_{\|\boldsymbol{w}\|=1} J(\boldsymbol{w}) = \operatorname*{arg\,max}_{\|\boldsymbol{w}\|=1} \hat\sigma^2_{\boldsymbol{w}}.$$ Minimizing mean squared projection distance is identical to maximizing projected variance.

Proof

Expand $\|\boldsymbol{x} - (\boldsymbol{x}\cdot\boldsymbol{w})\boldsymbol{w}\|^2 = \boldsymbol{x}^\top\boldsymbol{x} - 2(\boldsymbol{x}\cdot\boldsymbol{w})^2 + (\boldsymbol{x}\cdot\boldsymbol{w})^2\|\boldsymbol{w}\|^2 = \|\boldsymbol{x}\|^2 - (\boldsymbol{x}\cdot\boldsymbol{w})^2$ using $\|\boldsymbol{w}\|=1$. Summing over $i$ and dividing by $n$: $J(\boldsymbol{w}) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_i\|\boldsymbol{x}^{(i)}\|^2 - \frac{1}{n}\sum_i(\boldsymbol{x}^{(i)}\cdot\boldsymbol{w})^2 = C - \hat\sigma^2_{\boldsymbol{w}}$. Since $C$ does not depend on $\boldsymbol{w}$, minimizing $J(\boldsymbol{w})$ maximizes $\hat\sigma^2_{\boldsymbol{w}}$. □

This theorem is at the heart of why PCA is so canonical: two natural but seemingly different objectives — "maximize spread" and "minimize reconstruction error" — turn out to be mathematically identical. Any method that optimizes one automatically optimizes the other. The proof is also notable for its simplicity; the key step is merely completing the square of the Euclidean distance.

20.5.3 PCA vs. Least-Squares Regression

Intuition · The Critical Difference Between PCA and Least-Squares Regression

Both PCA and linear regression minimize a sum of squared errors, but they measure "error" in fundamentally different ways. In least-squares regression, one direction (the label $y$) is treated as special, and error is measured only as the vertical distance in the $y$-direction. In PCA, all directions are equal; error is measured as the perpendicular distance from each point to the fitting subspace. Geometrically, regression fits a line by dropping vertical lines from the data; PCA fits a line by dropping perpendicular lines. The computational consequence: regression requires only solving a linear system ($O(d^3)$), while PCA requires an eigendecomposition (also $O(d^3)$ but with a larger constant). The conceptual consequence: PCA produces the line that best approximates the cloud of points as a geometric object; regression produces the function that best predicts $y$ from the other features.

20.6 Applications: Eigenfaces and Gene PCA

PCA is one of the most widely applied techniques across science and engineering. Two applications deserve special attention: eigenfaces (a historically important method in computer vision that builds direct intuition for what principal components represent) and population genetics (a striking modern application where PCA rediscovers the geography of Europe from DNA alone).

20.6.1 Eigenfaces

Suppose our design matrix $X \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d}$ contains $n$ grayscale face images, each flattened to a vector of $d$ pixels ($d = 200 \times 200 = 40{,}000$ for a 200×200 image). After centering (subtracting the mean face $\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}$), PCA computes eigenvectors of $X^\top X$. These eigenvectors — when reshaped to $200 \times 200$ images — are called eigenfaces.

Definition · Eigenfaces and Face Reconstruction

Given $n$ centered face images (rows of $X$) and eigenvectors $\boldsymbol{v}_1, \ldots, \boldsymbol{v}_d$ of $X^\top X$ sorted by descending eigenvalue, the $j$-th eigenface is $\boldsymbol{v}_{d-j+1}$ reshaped to pixel dimensions. Any face $\boldsymbol{x}$ (after centering by $\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}$) is approximated by its $k$-component reconstruction: $$\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_k = \bar{\boldsymbol{x}} + \sum_{j=1}^{k} (\boldsymbol{x} - \bar{\boldsymbol{x}}) \cdot \boldsymbol{v}_{d-j+1}\bigl)\,\boldsymbol{v}_{d-j+1}.$$ As $k$ increases from 1 to $d$, the reconstruction improves monotonically; at $k = d$ it is exact. In practice, $k \approx 50$–$150$ eigenfaces suffice to recognize faces reliably.

Figure 20.7. Illustration of the eigenfaces concept. Top row: the mean face $\bar{\mathbf{x}}$ (leftmost, shown as a grayscale image) and the first four eigenfaces $\mathbf{v}_1$–$\mathbf{v}_4$ (shown as red-blue images with magnitude proportional to coefficient; red/blue indicate positive/negative deviations from the mean). Eigenvalues $\lambda_1 = 3.8, \lambda_2 = 2.1, \lambda_3 = 1.4, \lambda_4 = 0.9$ indicate decreasing importance. Bottom row: reconstructions of a face at $k = 1$ through $k = 5$ principal components, showing progressively finer detail as more eigenfaces are added.

The eigenfaces representation reveals what PCA "sees" in face data. The first eigenface typically captures global illumination variation (overall brightness). The next few capture changes in pose (left/right, up/down tilts). Subsequent eigenfaces capture finer details like glasses, hair color, and individual expression. This decomposition shows that faces, despite living in a $d = 40{,}000$-dimensional pixel space, effectively vary along only $\sim 100$ meaningful dimensions — the rest is pixel noise.

Example · Face Compression via Eigenfaces

Setup. You have $n = 500$ face images each of size $200 \times 200$ (40,000 pixels). You run PCA and find that the top $k = 100$ eigenfaces capture 92% of the variance ($\text{EVR}(100) = 0.92$).

Compression rate. Original storage per image: 40,000 floats. Compressed storage: 100 principal coordinates + the $k = 100$ eigenfaces (stored once, $100 \times 40{,}000$ floats). For the 500 images, the per-image coordinate vectors cost $500 \times 100 = 50{,}000$ floats total, compared to $500 \times 40{,}000 = 20{,}000{,}000$ floats original. The compression ratio is 400:1 on the per-image storage (ignoring the one-time cost of the eigenfaces themselves).

Tradeoff. The reconstructed images will not be pixel-perfect — 8% of the variance (primarily noise and fine texture) is discarded. For face recognition tasks, this is often acceptable; for forensic or medical imaging, it may not be.

20.6.2 Population Genetics and Geography

One of the most striking applications of PCA appeared in a 2008 paper in Current Biology (Novembre et al.). The study collected genetic data from 2,547 people across Europe, measuring 309,790 single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) — binary indicators of genetic variants at specific positions in the genome. Each person is represented by a $309{,}790$-dimensional binary vector.

PCA was applied to this dataset, projecting the genetic data down to 2 dimensions. The result was extraordinary: the two principal coordinates of each person's genome closely matched that person's geographic origin within Europe. The first principal component correlated with east–west position; the second with north–south position. Without any knowledge of where people lived or what countries they came from, PCA recovered a genetic map that closely mirrored the physical map of Europe.

Data Science Application · What Gene PCA Tells Us

The genetics-geography correspondence arises because people who live geographically close to each other share more recent common ancestors, and hence more similar genomes, than people who live far apart. PCA identifies the principal axes of genetic variation, which happen to align with geographic axes because migration, geography, and gene flow all have strong spatial structure. The result is not circular — PCA does not use geographic information. It purely maximizes variance in the 309,790-dimensional genetic space and the result coincidentally encodes geography. This example beautifully illustrates PCA's power as a structure-discovery tool: the structure (geography) was always implicit in the data; PCA makes it explicit.

Warning · PCA May Discard Predictively Important Directions

The most important caveat about using PCA as a preprocessing step for supervised learning: the directions of maximum variance are not necessarily the directions of maximum predictive power. A feature dimension with low variance might be the most discriminative for the task at hand. For example, suppose a disease is caused by a rare but highly penetrant genetic variant that appears in only 5% of the population — this will have low variance but enormous predictive importance. PCA would likely discard it. When labels are available, supervised dimensionality reduction methods (Linear Discriminant Analysis, or the more modern supervised PCA variants) should be considered alongside or instead of standard PCA.

✦ Chapter Summary

Principal Components Analysis is the canonical unsupervised dimensionality reduction algorithm. Given a centered design matrix $X \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d}$, PCA computes the eigenvectors of $X^\top X$ (or equivalently the sample covariance $\hat\Sigma = X^\top X/n$), retains the $k$ eigenvectors with the largest eigenvalues, and projects all data points onto the resulting $k$-dimensional subspace. Two independent optimization arguments justify this choice: (1) the selected subspace maximizes the variance of the projected data (via Rayleigh quotient maximization), and (2) it minimizes the mean squared distance from each data point to the subspace (reconstruction error). These two criteria are mathematically identical. PCA finds wide application as a preprocessing step before supervised learning, as a visualization tool for high-dimensional data, and as a scientific instrument for discovering latent structure — from eigenfaces in computer vision to population structure in genomics.

UNSUPERVISED LEARNING

No labels; goal is discovering data structure. Three main forms: clustering (discrete grouping), dimensionality reduction (finding low-$d$ structure), density estimation (fitting a distribution $p(\boldsymbol{x})$). PCA is the canonical dimensionality reduction algorithm.

ORTHOGONAL PROJECTION

For unit vector $\boldsymbol{w}$, the projection of $\boldsymbol{x}$ is $\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}} = (\boldsymbol{x}\cdot\boldsymbol{w})\boldsymbol{w}$. For orthonormal basis $\boldsymbol{v}_1,\ldots,\boldsymbol{v}_k$: $\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}} = \sum_i (\boldsymbol{x}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}_i)\boldsymbol{v}_i = VV^\top\boldsymbol{x}$. Projection matrix $P = VV^\top$ is idempotent ($P^2 = P$) and symmetric.

PCA ALGORITHM

Center $X$; optionally normalize; form $X^\top X$; eigendecompose; sort eigenvectors by eigenvalue; keep top $k$; compute $Z = XV_k \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times k}$ (principal coordinates). Centering is mandatory; normalization depends on whether features have different units.

SAMPLE COVARIANCE

The MLE estimate of the Gaussian covariance is $\hat\Sigma = \frac{1}{n}X^\top X$ (for centered $X$). PCA eigenvectors = principal axes of the fitted Gaussian. Eigenvalues $\lambda_j$ equal $n$ times the variance along $\boldsymbol{v}_j$. Total variance $= \text{tr}(\hat\Sigma) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_j \lambda_j$.

RAYLEIGH QUOTIENT

$R(M,\boldsymbol{w}) = \boldsymbol{w}^\top M\boldsymbol{w}/\|\boldsymbol{w}\|^2$. For symmetric $M$ with eigenvalues $\lambda_1 \leq \cdots \leq \lambda_d$: $\lambda_1 \leq R \leq \lambda_d$, with $R = \lambda_j$ iff $\boldsymbol{w} \propto \boldsymbol{v}_j$. PCA maximizes projected variance by maximizing $R(X^\top X, \boldsymbol{w})$.

MINIMUM RECONSTRUCTION ERROR

Mean squared projection distance $J(\boldsymbol{w}) = C - \hat\sigma^2_{\boldsymbol{w}}$ where $C$ is the total variance. Therefore $\operatorname*{arg\,min} J = \operatorname*{arg\,max} \hat\sigma^2$: maximizing projected variance and minimizing reconstruction error are equivalent. Proof: complete the square of $\|\boldsymbol{x} - \tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}\|^2$.

SCREE PLOT

Explained variance ratio: $\text{EVR}(k) = \sum_{j=d-k+1}^d \lambda_j / \sum_j \lambda_j$. The scree plot of EVR vs. $k$ guides selection of $k$. Look for the "elbow" or choose $k$ where EVR exceeds 90%. Cross-validation on a downstream task gives a more principled but costlier criterion.

PCA VS. REGRESSION

Both minimize sum of squared errors, but measure error differently. Regression measures vertical error in the $y$-direction (one privileged direction). PCA measures perpendicular distance from each point to the subspace (all directions equal). Regression requires a linear system solve; PCA requires eigendecomposition.

EIGENFACES

PCA on face images gives eigenvectors ("eigenfaces") that form a basis for the face space. Any face is approximated by $\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_k = \bar{\boldsymbol{x}} + \sum_{j=1}^k z_j \boldsymbol{v}_{d-j+1}$. With $k \approx 100$ eigenfaces (out of 40,000 pixels), face recognition remains reliable — faces lie near a $\sim 100$-dimensional manifold.

NORMALIZATION CHOICE

Normalize when features have different units (otherwise variance is scale-dependent and arbitrary). Do not normalize when features share units or when relative variance is meaningful (e.g., image pixels). Normalizing multiplies each column by $1/s_j$, making all features have unit empirical variance before PCA.

Exercise 20.1 · Projection Mathematics
Orthogonal Projection and the Projection Matrix

Let $\boldsymbol{v}_1 = (1/\sqrt{2}, 1/\sqrt{2})^\top$ and $\boldsymbol{v}_2 = (1/\sqrt{2}, -1/\sqrt{2})^\top$ in $\mathbb{R}^2$.

  1. Derivation. Prove that the projection matrix $P = \boldsymbol{v}_1\boldsymbol{v}_1^\top$ satisfies $P^2 = P$ (idempotent) and $P^\top = P$ (symmetric). Interpret $P^2 = P$ geometrically: what does it mean to project twice?
  2. Computation. For the point $\boldsymbol{x} = (3, 1)^\top$, compute the projections $\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_1 = (\boldsymbol{x}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}_1)\boldsymbol{v}_1$ and $\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_2 = (\boldsymbol{x}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}_2)\boldsymbol{v}_2$. Verify that $\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_1 + \tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_2 = \boldsymbol{x}$ and that $\|\boldsymbol{x}\|^2 = \|\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_1\|^2 + \|\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_2\|^2$ (Parseval's identity for orthonormal bases).
  3. Interpretation. What is the 2D projection onto the 2D subspace spanned by both $\boldsymbol{v}_1$ and $\boldsymbol{v}_2$? Explain why projecting onto a complete orthonormal basis in $\mathbb{R}^d$ recovers the original vector exactly.
  4. ML connection. In PCA with $k = d$ (using all principal components), what happens to the reconstruction $\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}} = VV^\top\boldsymbol{x}$? When $k < d$, what information is lost, and why is this acceptable for most downstream tasks?

  2. Computation. For $k=1$, which direction $\boldsymbol{v}^*$ maximizes $\hat\sigma^2_{\boldsymbol{w}} = \boldsymbol{w}^\top(\frac{1}{n}X^\top X)\boldsymbol{w}$? What is the maximum projected variance? Compute $\text{EVR}(1)$, $\text{EVR}(2)$, $\text{EVR}(3)$.

Exercise 20.3 · Reconstruction Error
PCA as Optimal Low-Rank Approximation for Image Compression

You have $n = 1000$ grayscale $10 \times 10$ images of handwritten digits ($d = 100$ pixels). After centering, the eigenvalues of $X^\top X$ (sorted descending) are $\lambda_{100} = 5000, \lambda_{99} = 3000, \lambda_{98} = 1500, \lambda_{97} = 800$, and the remaining 96 eigenvalues sum to 700.

  1. Derivation. Prove that the mean squared reconstruction error for the best $k$-dimensional projection is $\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{d-k}\lambda_j$ (the sum of the $d-k$ discarded eigenvalues). Use the equivalence $J(\boldsymbol{w}) = C - \hat\sigma^2_{\boldsymbol{w}}$ from Section 20.5.
  2. Computation. Compute the mean squared reconstruction error for $k = 1, 2, 3, 4$ components and the EVR at each. At what $k$ do you cross 90% explained variance?
  3. Interpretation. Original storage: 100 floats per image. With $k=4$ principal coordinates, how many floats must be stored per image (ignoring the fixed cost of the $k$ eigenvectors)? What compression ratio does this achieve?
  4. Extension. Show that the matrix $XV_kV_k^\top$ (the rank-$k$ approximation of $X$) minimizes $\|X - A\|_F^2$ over all $n \times d$ matrices $A$ of rank at most $k$, where $\|\cdot\|_F$ is the Frobenius norm. This is the Eckart–Young theorem — state it precisely and outline the proof using the SVD.

Exercise 20.5 · Eigenfaces and Dimensionality
Face Recognition System Design via PCA

You are building a face recognition system with $n = 500$ training images of $200 \times 200$ pixels ($d = 40{,}000$). You want to project to $k$ dimensions before running nearest-neighbor classification.
